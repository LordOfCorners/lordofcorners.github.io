Poland 2026

Fifteen days in Poland. My grandfather, Adam Kahane, was born outside Jasło in 1922 and left for good in 1947 — one of the few in his family who survived the war. In his nineties he wrote it all down, and I spent many weekends at his house helping him type and publish My Journey: Memoirs of a Lucky Man. I recently got Polish citizenship through him. After the two year process, this trip is the last step — my first time in the country.

Notes on the Ground

  • Sun Sep 6 · QueerMuzeum Warszawa — Marszałkowska 83, near the Palace of Culture. Poland's first LGBTQ+ museum and the first in post-communist Europe, opened December 2024 by Lambda Warszawa; around 150 objects drawn from an archive Lambda has kept since the 1940s. Free, and open only three days a week — Sunday 12:00–16:00 is the only window on the whole trip. A free English guided tour runs 12:00–13:30. Individuals don't need to book, but it's volunteer-run and the room holds about forty, so email kontakt@queermuzeum.pl ahead. Fits either side of the Uprising Museum.
  • Sun Sep 13 · Podgórze — Schindler's ends around 11:45. Krakus, an old-school bar mleczny at ul. Limanowskiego 16, is a few minutes away and open 9am–4pm on Sundays. The Eagle Pharmacy is Wed–Sun only, so this Sunday is the one realistic window for it on the whole trip; 7 minutes' walk, 45 min to an hour and a quarter inside.
  • Mon Sep 14 · Wieliczka — a constant 14–17°C underground, so everyone needs a jacket whatever the weather above. The descent is around 800 steps; the way out is by lift. Closed comfortable shoes, no large bags. Meeting point Daniłowicza 10, about 30 minutes out of Kraków.
  • Tue Sep 15 · Auschwitz, if we go — the tour is 3h45 across Auschwitz I and Birkenau, finishing around 3:15pm, and arrival 30 minutes early is required for airport-style security. The Lajkonik bus from MDA (Bosacka 18, right beside Kraków Główny) at 9:20am lands at the Museum car park at 10:45 — leave the Warszauer by taxi around 8:45. The train works too (IC 3402, 9:53 → Oświęcim 10:36) but leaves the last mile on foot or by local bus, which matters more on the way back after four hours standing; returning is Lajkonik from the car park, or a train connection into Kraków around 5:18pm. Nothing bigger than a shoebox goes inside — 5 zł storage on site, card only. The break between the two camps is only about 15 minutes, so bring something you can eat quickly. Most of the visit is outdoors on uneven ground.
  • Wed Sep 16 · Zakopane — check-in at the Aries isn't until 3pm, so leave the bags at reception and go straight out. Krupówki is a 5-minute walk. Gubałówka needs no booking — walk up and buy a ticket.
  • Thu Sep 17 · Morskie Oko — arrange the ride to the TPN pickup with the front desk the night before; Uber and Bolt are unreliable that early. The e-bus goes all the way to Włosienica, leaving a 1.5km walk to the lake rather than the usual two-hour approach. The PTTK hut at the lake is cash only. My optional side trip to Czarny Staw pod Rysami is 1.5–2 hours round trip, steep but moderate — not the Rysy summit, which is a serious climb.
  • Fri Sep 18 · Kasprowy — pack the night before, check out early, bags with reception. Taxi to Kuźnice is about 8 minutes; be there by 9:10am for the ID check, and expect a short boarding queue even with a timed ticket. I'm the named passenger on all three tickets and the one who gets verified — bring my passport. The ticket is a fixed 1h40 at the summit; lunch at Poziom 1959 up top, full service from 10am. Back for bags, then the 2:00pm train.
  • Cash — needed for tips throughout, and for the PTTK hut.
My grandfather wrote his memoir in his nineties and called it My Journey: Memoirs of a Lucky Man. The full story is there. These are the pieces of it that belong to a few of the places on this trip.
I. Łódź Sep 10–12
Adam was born in 1922 and spent his first five years in Łódź, where his father Jacob ran a profitable pharmacy and a laboratory making bases for ointments and face creams. After the divorce his mother took him to Jasło, and Jacob stayed.

Adam started visiting on his own at about ten. Jasło was two hundred miles away and the trip had three train transfers. There wasn't much for him to do in Łódź. He'd ride the streetcar to the pharmacy and watch the pharmacists mix medicines, or go with his father to the café, where he was introduced as the successor to the throne.

From the memoir

"My father stayed in Łodz during the occupation. Luckily his pharmacy was in the area designated as ghetto and he operated it almost to the time of the final liquidation of the ghetto. I was told by an Auschwitz survivor that he died of a heart attack on the train to the death camp. He was only 54."

— Adam Kahane

Adam was deported to the Soviet Union in 1940 and didn't get back to Łódź until 1946. He went straight to the pharmacy, where his uncle Iziu — a pharmacist who had worked for Jacob before the war — had been keeping it.

From the memoir

"As I entered the pharmacy, I saw uncle Iziu. He kissed me and said, 'This place is yours. I will help you run it for a while.'"

— Adam Kahane

He ran his father's pharmacy for most of a year before selling it for $2,000 and leaving Poland. The shop was at ul. Limanowskiego 80 — called Aleksandrowska then — and the building is still a pharmacy today. The Judenrat's registry of ghetto inhabitants lists two addresses for him: Alexanderhofstrasse 40, apartment 9 — Limanowskiego 40 today, a few blocks down from the pharmacy — and Kilińskiego 88.

II. Kraków Sep 12–16 & 18–19
The rest of the family settled in Kraków after they got back from Kazakhstan, and Adam spent much of 1947 there with them before leaving the country that autumn. His own residence record lists ul. Świętego Sebastiana 5 as his Kraków address — a few minutes' walk from where we're staying in Kazimierz. Trains were still dangerous for Jews that year; from time to time they heard of passengers being pulled off and shot.
From the memoir

"We concluded that there is no future for us in communist Poland and decided to emigrate. The pharmacy was very profitable but I got only $2000, when I sold it... We left Poland in September 1947 after a year's stay."

— Adam Kahane

From Kraków the family took the train to Paris in the autumn of 1947, and a year later a chartered propeller plane to New York. Adam came back once, in 1976, with his wife Gert.

III. The Mountains Sep 16–18
Adam learned to ski as a boy in the hills around Jasło — his aunt Hanka led the children out single file, and they hitched rides home behind peasants' sleds. He and his cousin Reggie were the only boys at their school with their own kayak, who played tennis in summer and skied in winter.
Cross-country skiing in a forest near Jasło.

Fifty years later, in Washington, that boyhood skiing became Ski Center. Adam and Gert opened it in 1959 and ran it for over half a century, my father took it over, and it closed a few years ago. Three days in the Tatras is the closest this trip gets to where it started.

IV. Jasło A possible day trip · Sep 15
Jasło is where Adam grew up, from five to seventeen — in his grandfather Józef Menasse's house on the Rynek, shared with his aunt Hanka's family. Her son Reggie, five months older, was his inseparable companion for the next twenty-five years: school in Jasło, nursing school in Kazakhstan, banking school in Moscow, and finally Columbia.
From the memoir

"My parents' divorce was a blessing for me because I exchanged the life of an only child in a dysfunctional family in a large industrial city for a life in a large close knit family in a pretty, small town full of aunts, uncles, and cousins."

— Adam Kahane
The Jasło Rynek in a prewar postcard.

Summers were spent on the Jasiołka — a kayak kept at a warehouse by the bridge, potatoes baked in the ashes of a fire, and from thirteen on, a hundred-mile river trip every year, sleeping in barns.

From the memoir

"On the other bank of the river were a meadow and a small grove. It was so pretty and peaceful there. Many years later, when I wanted to think about something relaxing, I thought about this place."

— Adam Kahane
Adam and friends on the Jasiołka.

Jasło is also where most of the family died. Aunt Manka — a doctor, decorated by a German general in the First World War for saving a squad of gassed soldiers — believed that citation meant she had nothing to fear from the Germans, and was shot after intervening for her arrested ex-husband; he was beaten to death at the Gestapo. Their daughters Renatka and Jasia, Adam's closest companions, were driven out of town, ordered to push the car, and shot from behind. They were twenty-one and eighteen. Uncle Michał had moved his family from Łódź to Jasło believing they were safer among people they knew; early in 1940 a Catholic relative offered to get them Aryan papers.

From the memoir

"Michał refused saying that he feels safe in Jaslo and could not live a life of lies."

— Adam Kahane

He and his family were sent to Treblinka, together with Uncle Dulek — who was marched to the station past the house of one of Adam's teachers and called out to him in Latin: Ave Caesar, morituri te salutant.

Caption to a family photograph, Jasło

"Of all of the children shown in this picture, I am the only one left."

— Adam Kahane

The town was almost entirely destroyed in the war. In 1976 Adam and Gert stood in front of the rebuilt house on the Rynek, its ground floor by then a state butcher's shop.

Adam and Gert at the rebuilt family house, circa 1976.
